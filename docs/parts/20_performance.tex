\section{Performance and memory}
\label{sec:perf}

\subsection{Cost model}

The per-chunk cost is
\begin{equation}
  C_{\text{chunk}} =
  C_{\text{flatten}} + C_{\text{capacity}} + C_{\text{reserve}} +
  C_{\text{upload}} + C_{\text{dispatch}} + C_{\text{read}} +
  C_{\text{publish}}.
\end{equation}
Flattening and capacity accounting scale with the number of nodes in the
neighbourhood; the dispatch scales with the number of scan cells and active
edges.  The host reads ten integers after one fence; there is no counting
submission between them.  The one retryable cost is a defensive span
overflow, which reuses the uploaded input and only re-reserves the chunk.

averages every 256 chunks when \code{VULKAN_SETUP_DIAG=1}.  The following
windows were captured from the earlier two-stage path; they are retained as
a baseline because the single-pass path removes the first submission and
the intermediate raw-key traffic, so it cannot be slower for the same
inputs.

\subsection{Throughput}

The geometry pass is one invocation per scan cell.  For a chunk with about
900 scan cells and 3.4k nodes, the baseline 8--19 ms includes the host-side
flatten and upload, two submissions, and the fence wait.  The current path
removes the counting submission; its remaining cost is dominated by the
host-side flattening plus the adaptive per-cell branch divergence and break
collection, not by the QEF solve or the index append.

\subsection{Per-chunk utilization profiling}

Every build records a \code{ChunkMeshProfile}: chunk id, layer, LoD, cross
edges, the maximum per-edge segment bound, reserved vertex/index capacities,
distinct payloads actually written, the published vertex extent, appended
indices, overflow counts, dispatch attempts, and whether the span grew.
Because the counters are the ones the build already reads back, profiling
adds no GPU readback and no extra traversal.  The registry keeps the latest
profile per \code{(layer, chunk)} and caches a sorted snapshot, so the
overlay pays no sort unless a build changed the set.

The overlay reports aggregate totals plus per-chunk minimum, mean, median,
and maximum for vertex utilization, index utilization, and the byte-weighted
combined utilization
\begin{equation}
  U_{\text{combined}} =
  \frac{64\,N_v + 4\,N_i}{64\,C_v + 4\,C_i},
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the actual count and $C$ the reserved capacity.  It also counts
chunks below 25\,\% combined utilization and chunks with an overflow or
growth event.  The I/O boundary calls \code{logMemoryUtilization()}, which
prints the aggregate; setting \code{VULKAN\_CHUNK\_PROFILE} additionally
prints one line per chunk from that path and from the periodic stream
diagnostic.

\subsection{Memory budget}

The reference configuration reserves:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Resource & Size & Notes\\
\midrule
Solid vertex pool & 768 MB & 64 B $\times$ 12M elements\\
Solid index pool & 192 MB & 4 B $\times$ 48M elements\\
Water vertex pool & 96 MB & 64 B $\times$ 1.5M elements\\
Water index pool & 96 MB & 4 B $\times$ 24M elements\\
Vegetation concat A/B & 256 MB $\times$ 2 & 8M instances $\times$ 32 B\\
Vegetation baked A/B & 32 MB $\times$ 2 & one float per instance\\
Vegetation scratch & 80 MB & 2 $\times$ 10 $\times$ 131k $\times$ 32 B\\
Texture arrays & 720 / 747 MB & five maps, 28 layers\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The pools are preallocated once.  A publish only carves or frees a span;
it never allocates a Vulkan buffer.  This is why the steady-state path has
zero \code{vmaCreateBuffer} calls and why a streaming burst cannot
fragment the allocator.

\subsection{Streaming behaviour}

Chunks are drained at a controlled rate (16 per frame in the scene
publisher, 10 per frame for vegetation).  A full drain would submit
hundreds of topology builds at once; on a 4 GB iGPU that trips the amdgpu
watchdog.  The rate limit keeps the submit queue bounded and spreads the
GPU work over frames.  The vegetation and fire effects lag the solid
publish by one or two frames; that is acceptable because the chunk is not
visible until its draw entry is published.

\subsection{Failure costs}

The expensive failure is a span-overflow retry: it releases the chunk's
reservation and re-runs the geometry dispatch with the exact reported
capacities.  The cross-edge bound is a topology bound, so this should be
rare; it is bounded to three attempts.  A fixed break-list overflow or an
invalid slot fails the chunk instead of retrying.  A pool exhaustion is a
sizing bug: the pools are preallocated for the worst measured scene, and a
new scene must bump the budget deliberately.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The pipeline is not ``free''.  It pays:
\begin{itemize}
\item a per-chunk flatten and cross-edge capacity pass;
\item one upload of the node neighbourhood;
\item one submission and one fence wait;
\item a small host read of ten counters;
\item a per-chunk span reservation and descriptor update.
\end{itemize}
It saves:
\begin{itemize}
\item a GPU$\rightarrow$CPU vertex readback;
\item a CPU-side QEF solve for every vertex;
\item a host-side triangle append and a staging upload;
\item a CPU-to-GPU copy of the index stream.
\end{itemize}
On a discrete GPU the readback and staging are the dominant costs; on an
integrated GPU they are even more expensive because the memory is shared.
The measured topology cost is the price of the split, and it is paid on
worker threads, not on the render thread.
